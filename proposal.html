The problem

Every week, someone decides 105 screen-slots per venue by hand.

Who

The programming manager

Chooses film, hall and time for next week's schedule before it is published, within distributor show commitments.

Why it matters

Seats are perishable

A popular film in a small hall turns people away. A weak film in a big hall leaves seats empty. Both are lost for good.

Expected value

More admissions, same assets

Same films, halls and staff; only the arrangement changes. The size of the gain is not yet known for your venues; measuring it is the point of the study.

Scope of the first step: one venue, one week at a time, film slate and show limits given. Film booking, pricing, and multi-venue planning come later.

Your data

Purchase records tell us a lot, but not everything a schedule needs.

We can see

We cannot see

Options considered

Four ways to help. We recommend the second.

A

Rule of thumb

Shows in proportion to last week's sales, biggest film in the biggest hall. Simple, but blind to time-of-day fit.

Used as our baseline.

B · recommended

Estimate + optimize

Estimate demand per film and time slot, then compute the best arrangement under your rules. Exact, explainable, runs offline.

Audience overlap

From records to a schedule your manager can defend.

Inputs, processing, outputs, workflow
The optimization, precisely

An integer program, solved exactly as a network flow.

maximize Σ v(f,h,d,s) · x(f,h,d,s)
subject to
 Σf x ≤ 1 one film per hall and time
 minf ≤ Σ x ≤ maxf show limits
 Σh x ≤ 1 never two halls at once
 x = 0 if runtime + cleaning > slot fit
 x ∈ {0,1}
v = E[min(demand, seats)] expected tickets

Why this method. About 7105 ≈ 1088 possible weeks, so trying all is impossible. The rules are nested capacity limits, which makes the problem a min-cost flow: its best solution is automatically whole-numbered and found exactly in milliseconds. Unlike a greedy rule, the flow can move a film out of one slot when its limited shows are worth more elsewhere.

One unit of flow = one screening. Example from the demo, Saturday: the family film earns 130 in Hall 1 at 10:00, the blockbuster only 78, so the flow sends the blockbuster to Hall 2 in the morning and Hall 1 in the evening.

Optimization strategy: formulation, graph, rationale
Watching it converge

Prices on shows steer the plan to the proven optimum.

  1. Give every film a price per show (start at 0).
  2. Plan each time slot on its own for tickets minus prices: an upper bound no schedule can beat.
  3. Repair it into a valid week: a real schedule.
  4. Raise prices of films over their limit, lower those under, repeat.

After 74 rounds the gap is 0.5 tickets: the plan is proven best, matching the exact network-flow answer (6,836).

Bonus for the manager: the final prices are shadow prices. Allowing one more PAPER LANTERNS show (cap 28) would add about 25 expected tickets; allowing one more NEON TIDE show (cap 28) would add about 8 expected tickets. That tells you which distributor limits cost you most.

The comparison point

How the rule of thumb builds its week.

  1. Score each film by its tickets in the last history week.
  2. Quota = open screen-slots × share of all scores, clipped to min-max, adjusted to sum to the open slots.
  3. Fill 19:00, 22:00, 16:00, 13:00, 10:00; biggest hall first; all days.
  4. Pick the top-scoring film with quota left that fits and is not already on at that time.

Sensible and common in spirit, but blind to time-of-day demand. It is our proposed reference; your real practice is unknown and would replace it in the trial.

What we learned

The model must not over-trust options nobody has tried.

In a simulated cinema where the true demand is known, trusting the model fully promised 8,965 tickets but really sold 6,593, less than the rule of thumb (6,689). The optimizer had chased guesses about film-time combinations never screened.

With a caution setting, the plan sold 7,711 in the same simulation.

simulation This checks the mechanism only. Real effects on your venues are untested; the caution level must be set in the replay study and trial.

Evaluation plan

Three stages, each with a clear way to stop.

Stage 0 · data audit

Receive records and screening log for one venue. Measure sell-outs, empty screenings, coverage.

Continue if the screening log exists with halls and seats.
Stop if it cannot be provided (fall back to demand analytics only).

Stage 1 · offline replay

Rebuild past weeks. Compare tool plans with what was actually screened, on the slots both chose.

Continue if estimates beat simple averages on held-out weeks.
Stop if overlap is too small to judge, or the tool loses.

Stage 2 · advisory trial

Alternate weeks: manager's own plan vs tool-assisted plan. Track admissions per show, sell-outs, overrides.

Adopt if tool weeks beat own weeks by the margin agreed beforehand.
Revise or stop otherwise.

Thresholds and trial length are agreed with you before any trial data is collected; they depend on how much admissions vary week to week, which the audit measures.
