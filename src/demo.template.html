Please confirm the data contract. We treat the six-field specification as the fixed and complete export. If fields are renamed, removed or change meaning (for example purchase time instead of screening time, or pre-aggregated rows), validation will reject the file or, worse, estimates will silently degrade.
GAP: there is no real data. The brief gives a record specification, not a dataset; size, period, quality and current practice are unknown. Every row on this page is generated. Real records of this venue would replace the generator one for one. Demand could be predicted more accurately with information beyond the six fields: sales of other cinemas showing the same film, each film's national box-office results (film success), pre-sales and booking curves, social-media reach and audience retention (trailer views, mentions, review scores), the release and holiday calendar, weather, competitor schedules and marketing spend. These are out of scope for the first step because none is in the records, each needs a collection agreement, and their link to one venue's admissions is unproven. We simplify by generating history from two distributions:
  • Distribution 1 - demand per screening: negative binomial with mean = film strength x time-of-day shape x weekday factor (all fictional) and dispersion r. Ticket buyers arrive as a count (Poisson) whose rate varies from screening to screening (Gamma mixing); this mixture is the negative binomial, the same Poisson-Gamma pair used by the estimate (Lecture 2, conjugate pairs and posterior predictive).
  • Distribution 2 - tickets per purchase: categorical 1 to 6 tickets with fixed shares (30%, 45%, 10%, 10%, 3%, 2%; fictional). Sales = min(demand, seats) are split into purchases with this distribution.
The past schedule is chosen by a fictional programmer who knows roughly what sells (judgement noise set in Settings), so some film-time combinations are never tried, as in real records.

How the plan is made

Methods come only from the course's Lectures 0-4 (Bayesian statistics for the belief, linear programming with a robust value and duality for the decision). Every step runs in this page; nothing is pre-computed.

Sum purchases into screenings

Tickets of a screening \(y_i = \sum \text{passes\_bought}\) over its rows. A total equal to one of the venue's hall sizes is read as a sell-out [ASSUMPTION], because records carry no hall: it means "demand was at least \(y_i\)", and the estimate uses \(\max(y_i,\ \text{pattern})\) for it.

Fit the pattern by maximum likelihood

\[\mu^{\text{pattern}}_{f,s,d} = F_f \cdot S_s \cdot D_d\] Film strength \(F\), time-of-day index \(S\) and weekday index \(D\) maximise the Poisson likelihood of all screenings (each factor = observed / expected tickets over its rows, repeated until stable). It gives every film-time-day an estimate, even where the film never played. Lecture 2, MLE (p. 24).

Update the belief per cell with Bayes' rule

For film \(f\), time \(s\), day type \(t\): prior \(\theta \sim \text{Gamma}(k P,\ k)\) with mean \(P\) = pattern, worth \(k\) screenings; Poisson likelihood of the cell's screenings; posterior \[\theta \mid y \sim \text{Gamma}\Big(kP + \textstyle\sum_i y_i,\ \ k + \sum_i w_i\Big),\qquad \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y] = \frac{k}{k+\sum_i w_i}\,P + \frac{\sum_i w_i}{k+\sum_i w_i}\,\bar y\] a weighted average of pattern and own average (\(w_i\) = weekday exposure inside the day type). Lecture 2, Example 2.1 Poisson likelihood with Gamma prior (p. 20); posterior as a weighted average (pp. 16, 22).

Expected tickets in a given hall

Integrate over the belief instead of plugging in the mean: the posterior predictive of a Poisson-Gamma model is negative binomial, and expected sales in hall \(h\) are \[v_{f h d s} = \mathbb{E}\big[\min(D_{f s d},\ \text{seats}_h)\big],\quad D_{f s d} \mid y \sim \text{NegBin}\] so a bigger hall is worth more only when demand can exceed the smaller one. Lecture 2, "Full Bayes - integrate, don't plug in" (p. 31) and the predictive table (p. 32).

Be cautious where the records are silent

For a film with records, a time and day type it never played is an extrapolation, and an optimiser seeks out exactly the most optimistic extrapolations. Its demand is valued at the low end of a box, \((1-c)\) times the estimate (caution \(c\), default 0.5). With \(x \ge 0\) the worst case of a box is its lower corner, so the problem stays a linear program. A new release has no records at all; it is planned on the manager's own estimate and flagged. Lecture 1, robust LP (p. 25); "the optimiser will exploit the surrogate wherever it is wrong" (Lecture 0, p. 25; Lecture 1, p. 31).

Plan the week as a linear program

\(x_{f h d s} \in \{0,1\}\): film \(f\) in hall \(h\) on day \(d\) at slot \(s\). \[\max \sum v_{fhds}\,x_{fhds}\ \ \text{s.t.}\ \sum_f x_{fhds} \le 1,\ \ \sum_h x_{fhds} \le 1,\ \ \text{min}_f \le \sum_{h,d,s} x_{fhds} \le \text{max}_f,\ \ x_{fhds}=0 \text{ if runtime}_f + \text{changeover} > \text{slot}\] The 0/1 requirement is relaxed to \(x \ge 0\): the hall rows and the film rows form two nested families with 0/1 coefficients, so every vertex of the polyhedron is a whole schedule, and the LP optimum sits at a vertex. The page solves it with the simplex method and refuses any fractional result. Lecture 1, LP and the vertex property (p. 16).

Certify the plan and price the rules

LP duality gives an upper bound on every feasible week; when it equals the plan's value (and every reduced cost is \(\le 0\)), no better week exists for the stated formulation. The dual price of a minimum-show rule says how many expected tickets one more required show costs. Lecture 1, KKT and duality (pp. 37-42).

Compare with the rule of thumb

Baseline (a proposed reference, not the operator's known practice): score each film by last week's tickets per screening, give it shows in proportion to its score within its min/max, then fill the best-selling day-times first, biggest hall first, with the highest-scoring film that still has shows left, fits the slot and is not already on. Both plans are scored with the same expected-ticket function and the same rules.

What is real, what is mock, what is not built

REAL CALCULATION

Validation, aggregation, the maximum-likelihood pattern, the Bayes update, the negative-binomial expected sales, the robust value, the linear program (simplex), the integrality check, the duality certificate, the rule-of-thumb baseline, the backtest and every test case run live in this page from the settings above.

MOCK / FICTIONAL

All titles (invented parodies, no real film's data), the venue RIVERSIDE, halls, seats, runtimes, show limits, accounts and every purchase row. "True" demand exists only inside the generator; the panels marked MOCK score plans against it, which is impossible with real records. The +x% figures are fictional and say nothing about a real venue.

NOT IMPLEMENTED

Film booking (which films to acquire), pricing and revenue, several venues, mid-week changes, holidays and film ageing, a film in two halls at the same time, after-midnight shows, staff rosters, connection to a ticketing system, and the exploration (test-screening) policy proposed for later stages.

Reproduce a result

Results depend only on the settings: the same settings give the same fingerprint (shown under the title; default ). Randomness is used only by the fictional generator, through a seeded generator (mulberry32). The simplex uses fixed pivot rules (largest gain first; Bland's rule after a run of degenerate pivots), so ties are always broken the same way. The settings are also stored in the page address, so a reload restores them; "Reset to defaults" restores the defaults.

Checked offline (no network requests) in Chromium and Firefox desktop. No server, login, API key, CDN, web font or image file is used; fonts for formulas are embedded.
